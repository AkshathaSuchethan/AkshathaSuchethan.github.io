Case Study

Medlogics — booking a doctor without the hold music

A healthcare scheduling platform that lets patients search, compare, and book appointments in a few taps — replacing phone queues with a self-service flow built to hold up under real accessibility constraints, not just usability ones.

The problem, and the goal

Booking a doctor's appointment is still, for many patients, a high-friction task: long hold times, no visibility into real-time availability, and no self-service way to cancel or reschedule around a busy, unpredictable day. For working adults with caregiving responsibilities, that friction isn't just an inconvenience — it's a barrier to timely care.

The goal was to give patients a fast, trustworthy way to find the right doctor by specialty, symptom, or location; check real-time availability; and book, reschedule, or cancel an appointment in under two minutes — while making sure the experience held up for patients who rely on screen readers, keyboard navigation, or reduced-motion settings.

Understanding the user

Persona — Saanvi

A working parent with no time to wait in queue for care. Every added step between "I need a doctor" and "appointment confirmed" was a point of drop-off.

I conducted interviews and built empathy maps with working adults managing packed schedules — an audience whose needs are a useful proxy for how any time-constrained user interacts with a booking flow. Research surfaced three consistent pain points beyond "booking is slow."

Pain pointInsight
1 Time Users had no tolerance for queueing — digital or physical — and expected real-time slot visibility, not a request-and-wait model.
2 Trust & accessibility of information Patients struggled to evaluate doctors without credentials, reviews, or clear specialty match surfaced up front.
3 Flexibility No self-service path existed to cancel or reschedule during emergencies, forcing users back to a phone call.

Design system & foundations

Rather than designing screens in isolation, I built a small component library alongside the flows — the piece of this project I now treat as the most reusable asset.

Design tokens for color, spacing, and type scale kept contrast ratios and touch-target sizing consistent across every screen without manual rechecking. Reusable components — dropdowns, progress indicators, doctor cards, availability slots — were documented with default, hover, disabled, and error states for clean developer handoff.

This system-first approach meant that when usability testing surfaced the need for a new "recurring appointment" option, it could be built from existing components rather than a one-off screen.

From wireframe to prototype

Paper wireframes let me test the core booking logic — location, consultation type, doctor selection — before any visual design work began. Digital wireframes carried that structure forward, with two decisions shaped directly by research: a location and consultation-type dropdown surfaced early in the flow, so users could filter before browsing doctors rather than after, and a progress indicator giving users a clear sense of how close they were to a confirmed booking.

Usability testing

I ran two rounds of moderated usability testing, each closing a distinct gap.

Round 1 findings

  1. Users wanted to book an appointment in fewer steps.
  2. Users wanted more customization in how they filtered doctors.
  3. Users wanted doctor reviews visible before booking.

Round 2 findings

  1. Users still couldn't fully customize an appointment — care type, consultation type, and location together.
  2. Users wanted the option to save and repeat a previous booking — a recurring-appointment pattern.
6

Refining the design

Customization: after Round 1, I surfaced location, care type, and consultation type together on first load, rather than sequentially — cutting the number of screens needed to reach doctor selection. Trust signals: after Round 2, I added a doctor detail screen — credentials, specialty, and patient reviews — inserted before the booking confirmation step, so users could evaluate fit without leaving the flow.

Before — sequential filters
After — combined on first load

Accessibility pass: every revised screen was checked against WCAG 2.2 AA — color contrast, focus order, and screen-reader labeling on icon-only actions like bookmarking — rather than treated as a final-stage audit. Detailed doctor imagery included descriptive alt text, and the booking flow was fully operable via keyboard alone.

High-fidelity prototype

The final prototype delivered a streamlined path from search to confirmed booking, with the customization and review-checking behavior users had asked for built in from the start rather than bolted on.

Outcomes & reflection

"It made it so easy and quick to book an appointment" — I'd prefer it over calling.
Peer usability tester

What I'd carry forward: the clearest lesson from this project is that a component-first approach pays off the moment research surfaces a new need — the recurring-appointment feature was fast to design because the underlying system already existed. That's shaped how I approach every project since: build the reusable pieces early, so late-stage findings become configuration, not redesign.

Next steps, if continued today

  • A further round of testing focused specifically on the recurring-appointment and cancellation flows under real emergency conditions, not simulated ones.
  • Extending the component library into a documented, versioned design system usable across future Medlogics products.
  • Auditing the flow against WCAG 2.2 AAA where feasible, given the health-critical context.
